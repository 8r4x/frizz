Frizz — the cursive f

The written f, built as real geometry from the reference below. A cursive f is a lucky letterform here — it is already a curl, already one continuous thread, and already the first letter of the name. What it is not is a favicon, and this sheet is organised around that: how fine can the line be before 16px eats it, and what do you keep when it does.

The 16px rule

A 512-unit canvas maps to 16 px, so one pixel is 32 units. That single conversion decides what is drawable and rules out most of what a logo wants to be.

≥ 52uminimum stroke. Below this the mark dissolves into a smudge.
≥ 64uminimum ink gap or counter. Below this two features fuse, or a hole fills.
3elements across the width. That is the whole budget — 288 usable units buys no more.

The brief

The brief. Two narrow loops crossing once at the waist, thrown right at the top and left at the bottom, with a long crossbar — and real weight variation, heavy on the outside of the loops and fine through the crossings. Everything below is built from that description rather than traced over it.

Concepts

Signature shortlist

The written f, drawn as written

Two narrow loops crossing at the waist with a long rising crossbar — the reference, built as a real figure-eight rather than traced. The ascender throws right, the descender throws left, and the single crossing is what makes it read as handwriting instead of as a symbol.

At 16px. Fails, and it is not close. A 22-unit line is 0.69px at 16px, and the loops are 66 wide so their counters are 44 units — 1.4px. Both numbers are under the floor, so the letter thins to a grey smudge. This weight is a logo, not a favicon.

Risk. None as a logo — this is the most characterful mark in any of the three sheets. The risk is only that it cannot also be the favicon, so the brand needs two drawings rather than one.

Verdict. Use it big: README, site header, app splash, stickers. Pair it with a simplified companion for the tab.

Signature with swash shortlist

The same hand, with the tail thrown out to the right

The second reference. The descender does not close its loop; it runs out into a long horizontal tail, and a short bar comes in from the left to meet the stem. The tail gives the mark a baseline to sit a wordmark on.

At 16px. Fails at the same weight and for the same reason. The tail survives longest because it is the one horizontal run of ink.

Risk. The swash needs horizontal room, so this version does not fit a square tile as comfortably as the closed one — it wants to be a wide lockup.

Verdict. The better choice if the mark will usually appear beside the word Frizz rather than alone.

Signature with pen contrast shortlist

Thick where the pen presses, thin where it travels

The reference has real weight variation — the outsides of the loops are heavy, the crossing strokes are fine. This runs the same spine through a broad-nib model so the contrast is generated by the pen angle rather than drawn by hand.

At 16px. Fails, like every fine weight here, but it fails last: the heavy parts of the loops are the final thing to survive.

Risk. Contrast this high has to be redrawn for every size and weight — you cannot restroke your way to a bolder version.

Verdict. The most finished-looking of the three fine variants, and the one closest to the reference's actual texture.

Signature, bold shortlist

The same letter at a weight a tab can hold

The same letter at a 46 stroke. Weight and loop width have to move together — a heavier line needs wider loops or the counters close — so bolding also squares the proportions up from 3:1 toward 1.5:1.

At 16px. Holds, just. Loops widened to 112 leave 112-46 = 66 units of counter, and the 46 stroke is 1.4px. Legible rather than crisp.

Risk. Bolding a signature costs exactly the quality that made it one. The loops have to widen to keep their counters, and at that proportion the f starts reading as an 8 — visible in the render below.

Verdict. The honest compromise if there has to be one drawing. Compare it against running two.

Bold, no crossbar shortlist

The favicon companion

The bold body with the crossbar dropped and the loops opened further. Not a rival to the signature — the piece you show at 16px when the signature itself cannot go there.

At 16px. The best small render on this sheet. Losing the crossbar frees the whole width, so the loops open to 132 and the counters reach 80 units — 2.5px, the only comfortable margin here.

Risk. Plainly reads as an 8, not an f. That is survivable for a companion icon that always appears downstream of the real logo, and fatal for one that has to stand alone.

Verdict. The one to pair with Signature. A detailed logo plus a reduced icon is the normal way out of this, and this is the reduction.

Bold, knocked out in play

The companion reversed out of solid amber

The same reduced mark as a hole in a filled amber tile.

At 16px. Carries much more of the 16x16 than a thin line on near-black, so it holds its place in a tab strip instead of receding. Knocked-out counters read narrower than positive ones, so the letter is drawn a little heavier here.

Risk. Loud on every tab, forever.

Verdict. Worth testing head to head with the positive version once the form is settled.

Middleweight in play

Splitting the difference

A 34 stroke — heavier than the reference, lighter than the bold. Included to show where the cliff actually is rather than assuming it.

At 16px. Marginal, and this is where the cliff is. 34 units is 1.06px of stroke and the 88-wide loops leave 54 units of counter (1.7px): present, but soft rather than drawn.

Risk. Compromises tend to lose both arguments; this one is neither a signature nor a solid icon.

Verdict. Useful as a measurement, probably not as a choice.

Signature, bare in play

The written f with nothing crossing it

The fine body alone. Shows how much of the letter the crossbar is actually carrying — without it the figure-eight is elegant but stops being an f.

At 16px. Fails at this weight regardless.

Risk. Reads as an infinity sign or an ampersand flourish more than as a letter.

Verdict. Mostly a control, but a pretty one — it is the shape the wordmark could use as a divider or a bullet.

The pairing this points to

logo · 128px
favicon · 16px

Left: the signature, at the size it is meant for. Right: the hooked ƒ carrying the same letter at 16px, where the signature cannot go. Same hand, same amber, same tile — one is the logo, the other is the tab.

The finding, and it is not a close call. A written f cannot be the favicon. The reference's line is about 22 units on a 512 canvas, which is 0.69px at 16px, and its loops are narrow enough that their counters land at 1.4px. Both are under the floor. Bolding fixes the arithmetic and breaks the letter — the loops have to widen to keep their counters, and a widened figure-eight reads as an 8. Every bold variant above shows that happening.

The way out is two drawings, not one compromise. A detailed logo plus a reduced icon is ordinary brand practice. The best reduction is not a bolder signature — it is the hooked ƒ from the companion sheet, which is structurally sound at 16px and still visibly the same letter in the same hand.

One caveat on "16px", worth checking before you decide. The renders above are true 16 px. On a hi-dpi screen a browser fills the 16 px tab slot from the 32 px asset instead — and the signature is legible at 32 px, as its second evidence tile shows. So the verdict above is firm for a 1x display and may be too harsh for a 2x one. That is an assumption about favicon selection, not something measured here; test it in a real tab on a real Retina display before letting it change the decision.

What is not decided here. Colour is carried over from the existing mark (#e8b923 on #0d0e10) so the variants compare on form alone. The dark rounded tile is a contrast guarantee, not part of any mark.
